Indigenous Earthworks of Eastern North America

For more than 5,000 years, Indigenous communities in what is now the eastern United States shaped the land into mounds, enclosures, and plazas. These are places of ceremony, gathering, and political life. This project maps several of them, from the Archaic to the Mississippian period, and examines the false claims that once denied their builders credit.

    The “Mound Builder” Myth

    In the 1800s, many white Americans claimed that the earthworks were built by a vanished “lost race” of Mound Builders, not by the ancestors of Native Americans. Suggested builders included wandering Israelites, Vikings and other far-fetched groups, and the stories spread in popular books and newspapers.

    The myth was not harmless. It coincided with the era of Indian Removal and helped justify it. If Native people had not built these monuments, settlers could treat them as recent arrivals with no deep claim to the land.

    The evidence never supported the myth. Ephraim Squier and Edwin Davis surveyed hundreds of earthworks for the Smithsonian in 1848. Their maps were valuable, but they doubted Native authorship. In 1894, Cyrus Thomas of the Smithsonian's Bureau of Ethnology published a large field study concluding that the mounds were built by the ancestors of Native American peoples.

    Recognizing and breaking down fraudulent claims of the origins of these works is pivotal to fully appreciate the complexity of past Indigenous peoples.
